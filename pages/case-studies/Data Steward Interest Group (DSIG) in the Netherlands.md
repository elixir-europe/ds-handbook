<p class="drop-cap">The Data Steward Interest Group (DSIG) is an informal and inclusive community hub where data stewards and like-minded people in the Netherlands and abroad share their experiences. Anyone interested in data stewardship can join.</p>

## Why this case

In 2017, data stewardship was a relatively new profession in the Netherlands. Universities and university medical centres were hiring data stewards to help researchers make their data FAIR, but each steward was often the only one in their department. There was no obvious place to ask a peer at another institution: *"how did you solve this?"*

National and international groups worked on policy and competences for data stewards. What was missing was a hands-on place for practical matters, where data stewards learn directly from each other. The DSIG started as a collaboration between data stewards at university medical centres and the Dutch Techcentre for Life Sciences (DTL) to fill that gap.

## What we did

The DSIG began as a small, in-person meeting in the life sciences, every other month. During COVID-19 it moved online, and it has stayed online since. It gradually opened up to data stewards from all disciplines and from other countries. Today a community manager at Health-RI, the Dutch national health data infrastructure, organises the cross-domain meetings, and the three Thematic Digital Competence Centres (TDCCs) organise meetings for their own domains: life sciences and health, social sciences and humanities, and natural and engineering sciences. The DSIG meets roughly once a month, alternating general and domain meetings.

### A different chair every time

Each meeting is chaired by a different organisation. The chair picks the topic and shows what they are working on or struggling with. Most organisations volunteer. Others are asked because they raised a topic in an earlier meeting.

The community manager does everything else: the agenda, the registration form, the announcements and preparing the shared notes. That way the chair can focus on the content.

### The same agenda every time

Every meeting follows the same structure:

- an informal start with coffee or tea;
- an introduction round, so new people can introduce themselves;
- a list of participants with affiliation and contact details, for networking afterwards;
- *New and Newsworthy*, where anyone can share news or ask a question about data-related events and projects;
- *In Case You Missed It*: slides from the community manager with ten data-related news items from the past period that data stewards should know about;
- one or two presentations with discussion;
- feedback in the shared notes under *Keep, Add, Less & More*.

The part before the presentations matters most. In the introduction round, newcomers say what they do and why they joined, which often starts a conversation straight away. *New and Newsworthy* gives people time to ask each other questions and share how they solved something. This is where most of the peer learning happens.

Participants write the shared notes together during the meeting, and the notes are published afterwards with links to the presentations. If you missed a meeting, you can still catch up.

<aside class="margin-note">The notes start with "These notes are for you! Please add comments, +1s, gifs". People do.</aside>

The topics come from the chairs, so they follow what data stewards are working on at that moment. Some are about daily support, such as data management plans, privacy, data access committees and research data management (RDM) for students. Others show a tool or approach worth reusing, such as FAIR lesson plans, the Personal Health Train or The Turing Way. Chairs also bring in their own field: clinical data reuse, social sciences in the cloud, sports data, open hardware, research software and citizen science.

{% include callout.html type="tip" content="Keep one planning sheet: past meetings, topics in planning, loose ideas, and a contact person for each. It is the easiest way to always have a next chair lined up." %}

### Between meetings

The Slack community is where the DSIG lives between meetings. It is open to anyone working in or interested in data stewardship, in the Netherlands or abroad. Members ask each other questions, share events and job ads, post new guidance and tools, and point each other to what is happening in other countries. There is a lot going on in data stewardship, and this is one of the easiest ways to keep up. If you are a data steward, join us.

With the TDCCs, the DSIG also publishes *Spotlight on data stewards*: interviews with data stewards about their work and what they need from peers.

<blockquote class="pull-quote">
  <p>The two recurring issues challenging me as a data steward are determining data ownership and data sensitivity. It would be great to collaborate on these topics with other data stewards.</p>
  <cite>A data steward, in Spotlight on data stewards</cite>
</blockquote>

__Effort involved:__ the DSIG has no project funding of its own. It runs on part of a community manager's time at Health-RI, the coordinators at the TDCCs, and the time chairing organisations put in.

An informal community still takes work. Behind every meeting, the community manager finds and briefs a chair, prepares the agenda and registration, collects the *In Case You Missed It* items, sends the announcements and publishes the notes. Between meetings there is the Slack community and the planning of the next topics. Without someone who has this in their job, the meetings would stop.

{% include callout.html type="important" content="Low-threshold for participants does not mean no effort for organisers. Make sure someone has time for community management in their job, not on top of it." %}

## What changed

__Outputs and results__

The DSIG has met more than 50 times since 2017, and the notes of every meeting since 2018 are online. During the COVID-19 years, meetings had 50 to 75 participants. Recent meetings have around 30 to 35 registrations. There is no formal template, but anyone who wants to start a similar community can copy the approach, as it works very well.

__Evidence it worked__

- Chairs say chairing a meeting is useful and fun, mostly because of the engaged participants.
- In a 2023 poll, participants said the network helps them keep up to date and find collaborators and job offers. As one participant put it: *"being with like-minded"*.
- The group reaches well beyond the Netherlands. Data stewards from other countries join regularly, and their experiences add to every discussion.
- When the three TDCCs started, they chose to hold their data stewardship meetings within the DSIG instead of starting separate networks.

__Barriers encountered__

- Participants vary a lot from meeting to meeting, so it is hard to build on earlier discussions.
- Since the switch to monthly and domain-specific meetings, fewer people attend each one.
- Feedback is hard to collect. The *Keep, Add, Less & More* section often stays empty.
- The DSIG relies on a small coordination team: one community manager and the TDCC coordinators, who organise it as part of their job. Without those roles, nobody would keep the meetings going.

__Enablers__

- One organisation that takes care of the logistics, year after year.
- Participants who join out of personal interest rather than to represent their organisation, which keeps the tone informal.


## What we'd tell others

### What worked, and why

**Make chairing cheap.** With a fixed agenda and someone who handles the logistics, a chair only has to bring a topic. That is why organisations keep saying yes.

**Rotate the chair.** Every chair brings its own topic and network, so you see what is happening across the country.

**Make every meeting a good first meeting.** The introduction round, the participant list and the open notes make newcomers feel welcome. The same goes for people who missed the last three meetings.

{% include callout.html type="magpie" content="Make it easy to chair, easy to drop in and easy to catch up afterwards." %}

### What didn't work, and how we adapted

**Meeting only in person limited who could join.** Going online opened the group to the whole country and to colleagues abroad.

**One agenda for all disciplines became too broad.** We now alternate general meetings with domain meetings.

**More meetings meant smaller groups.** We pick broad topics for the general meetings, and we have found that a smaller group often has a better discussion.

### Transferability and scaling potential

The model works at institutional, regional or national level. The main condition is one organisation willing to facilitate for years. It fits best where data stewards are spread thinly over many organisations. Compare the [Data Support Network in Finland](/Data-Support-Network-Finland), a similar low-threshold model, and the [Nordic Data Stewardship Network](/Nordic_Data_Stewardship_Network_%28NDSN%29), which builds the same kind of exchange across borders.

If you want to start your own community, we are happy to help you get started.

### Sustainability and next steps

The DSIG depends on the community manager at Health-RI and the TDCC coordinators. The TDCCs are funded for ten years, which gives the network a stable base for now. Next, we want to link the DSIG to the new national training platform for data professionals, so people who take a course find their peers straight away.
